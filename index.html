How this tracker works

Evidence tiers

Cancelled, rejected, or expired claims are verified only with 1+ primary record (government/procurement document, court record, official statement, transparency portal) and 2+ independent news sources. Everything else is marked pending validation*, with its exact citation counts shown per record. News-only claims stay pending indefinitely, no matter how many outlets repeat the story. * Does not yet meet the evidence bar; shown while awaiting corroboration. Records not re-verified in 180 days drop to pending validation* automatically until re-confirmed. "Verified" here means "meets this evidence bar," not "absolute truth": read the underlying sources and decide for yourself.

Verify data integrity

Every dataset release ships a signed manifest (manifest.json) with the SHA-256 of agencies.json, a timestamp, and the commit it was taken at. This check recomputes the hash in your browser and compares it, so you can confirm the JSON you received matches what the maintainer released.

Honest scope: a matching hash proves the file matches the published release. It does not prove the hosting origin itself is uncompromised. For full provenance, check the manifest signature against the maintainer's published key and the public audit log of dataset changes.

How cancellations happen

  • Contracts have renewal dates. A renewal vote is the highest-leverage moment: the agency must publicly defend the system, and the tracker above shows when yours comes up.
  • What has worked elsewhere: public-records requests for the contract and audit logs, packing the council meeting, and citing neighboring towns that already cancelled.
  • 23+ jurisdictions cancelled or rejected Flock systems in 2026; roughly 98 have done so since 2021 (DeFlock tally via press reporting). The playbook is proven.

Spotting the cameras

  • Flock Falcon (ALPR): small bullet camera on a pole, usually with a solar panel and a boxy housing. Photographs every passing plate.
  • Flock Raven: audio gunshot-detection sensor, often co-located with Falcons.
  • Flock Condor: pan-tilt-zoom live video camera for intersections and lots.

Safety and scope

  • Flock-Off is for awareness and lawful civic action: mapping, records requests, public pressure, contract fights.
  • Do not touch, disable, or vandalize cameras. That carries criminal liability and shifts the debate from privacy policy to property damage.
  • Violence is never acceptable, under any circumstances. See the Legal tab for our full position and the verbatim laws behind it.

Privacy

  • No account, no cookies, no analytics, no fingerprinting. The site sets nothing in your browser and runs no third-party trackers.
  • Tips are public documents. If you submit a contract tip, what you type (agency, source link, notes) is stored and, once approved, shown publicly. Submit only public information. The form asks for no name, email, or private details, and none is stored.
  • The map talks to third parties. Camera data comes from the Overpass API and map tiles from OpenStreetMap, so using the map tab sends them your IP address and the area you view, the same as loading any online map. Your precise location, when you tap "Use my location", only centers the map on your device and is never sent anywhere.
  • Hosting. The site runs on Vercel, which keeps standard server request logs (IP, page, time) to operate the service; that cannot be switched off. Nothing is added on top: our code keeps no visitor logs, and the report endpoint uses your IP only in memory for rate limiting, never writing it to storage.

Legal

Violence is never acceptable

Flock-Off is a transparency project. We document public contracts between police agencies and Flock Safety so communities can debate them, file records requests, pack council meetings, and vote. That is the entire playbook.

Violence is not acceptable under any circumstances. Not against people, not against cameras, not against property, not as retaliation, not as protest. Specifically:

  • Do not damage, disable, deface, steal, or tamper with cameras or any related equipment.
  • Do not threaten, harass, stalk, or dox any person, including Flock Safety employees, police officers, elected officials, or private citizens.
  • Do not use information on this site to facilitate any of the above.

Anyone who commits violence or vandalism in the name of this cause is acting against this project, not for it. We will not defend it, excuse it, or characterize it as activism. Change the contract, not the hardware.

The law that protects this publication

Publishing truthful information about government contracts and matters of public concern is protected speech. Six authorities, quoted verbatim:

1. The First Amendment to the United States Constitution

Congress shall make no law respecting an establishment of religion, or prohibiting the free exercise thereof; or abridging the freedom of speech, or of the press; or the right of the people peaceably to assemble, and to petition the Government for a redress of grievances.

Source: National Archives transcription of the Bill of Rights, archives.gov/founding-docs/bill-of-rights-transcript

2. New York Times Co. v. United States, 403 U.S. 713, 714 (1971) (per curiam)

The Supreme Court refused to block publication of the Pentagon Papers, holding:

"Any system of prior restraints of expression comes to this Court bearing a heavy presumption against its constitutional validity."

Source: opinion text, law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/403/713

3. Bartnicki v. Vopper, 532 U.S. 514 (2001)

The Court held the First Amendment protects publishing truthful information about a matter of public concern:

"Parallel reasoning requires the conclusion that a stranger's illegal conduct does not suffice to remove the First Amendment shield from speech about a matter of public concern."

Source: opinion syllabus, law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/532/514

4. Cox Broadcasting Corp. v. Cohn, 420 U.S. 469, 495 (1975)

The Court held that states may not punish the publication of truthful information contained in official records open to public inspection:

"the States may not impose sanctions on the publication of truthful information contained in official court records open to public inspection."

Sources: opinion text, law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/420/469 and supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/420/469

5. The Florida Star v. B.J.F., 491 U.S. 524, 524-525 (1989)

The Court held that truthful, lawfully obtained information about a matter of public significance cannot be punished absent a state interest of the highest order:

"if a newspaper lawfully obtains truthful information about a matter of public significance then state officials may not constitutionally punish publication of the information, absent a need to further a state interest of the highest order."

Sources: official U.S. Reports PDF, govinfo.gov and law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/491/524

6. Smith v. Daily Mail Publishing Co., 443 U.S. 97, 102 (1979)

The Court stated the core rule later called the "Daily Mail principle":

"Our recent decisions demonstrate that state action to punish the publication of truthful information seldom can satisfy constitutional standards."

Sources: supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/443/97 and caselaw.findlaw.com/court/us-supreme-court/443/97

These protections cover the publication of truthful, lawfully obtained information. They do not create a right to obtain government records in the first place, and each case was decided on its own facts rather than as blanket immunity. Flock-Off stays inside these holdings by publishing only information gathered lawfully: public records requests, open meetings, and published documents. No hacking, no evasion of access controls.

When courts have ordered takedowns, and why those cases are different

The protection for publishing truthful information is strong, but it is not absolute. Courts have ordered published material removed in narrow circumstances. Each of those cases turned on something this project does not do:

7. United States v. Progressive, Inc., 467 F. Supp. 990, 996 (W.D. Wis. 1979)

A federal court enjoined a magazine from publishing technical details of hydrogen bomb design, holding the article fell "within the extremely narrow exception to the rule against prior restraint." The restraint rested on the Atomic Energy Act's criminal prohibition on disclosing nuclear "Restricted Data" found to pose grave, direct, immediate, and irreparable harm to the United States. Why it is different: no federal statute prohibits publishing public-records information about police contracts, and no court has found such publication a national-security harm.

Source: opinion text, law.justia.com/cases/federal/district-courts/FSupp/467/990/1376343

8. Universal City Studios, Inc. v. Corley, 273 F.3d 429, 435 (2d Cir. 2001)

The Second Circuit affirmed a permanent injunction barring the posting of, and knowingly linking to, the DeCSS DVD-decryption program under the DMCA's anti-trafficking provisions:

"The injunction primarily bars the Appellants from posting DeCSS on their web site and from knowingly linking their web site to any other web site on which DeCSS is posted. We affirm."

Why it is different: the injunction targeted distribution of a functional circumvention tool built to defeat copyright protection, not the republication of truthful facts drawn from public records.

Source: opinion text, law.justia.com/cases/federal/appellate-courts/F3/273/429/506315

9. DVD Copy Control Assn. v. Bunner, 31 Cal. 4th 864, 884 (2003)

A trial court ordered a website operator to remove posted DeCSS code on trade-secret grounds. The California Supreme Court held that such an injunction, when proper under trade-secret law, is no First Amendment violation:

"Because the injunction is content neutral and was issued because of Bunner's prior unlawful conduct, we conclude it is not a prior restraint and therefore does not violate the First Amendment."

The full story matters: on remand the injunction was reversed for failure of proof under trade-secret law, so the removal order did not ultimately stand. Why it is different: the entire theory was misappropriation of proprietary trade secrets the defendant knew were improperly acquired. Information drawn from public government records cannot be anyone's trade secret.

Source: filed opinion PDF, techlawjournal.com/courts2003/dvdcca_bunner/20030825.pdf

10. Harper & Row v. Nation Enterprises, 471 U.S. 539, 545 (1985)

This is the canonical case where a publisher lost, stated precisely: the relief was damages ($12,500), not a takedown order. The Nation published 300 to 400 words of verbatim excerpts from President Ford's unpublished memoir to scoop a licensed serialization, and the Court held the magazine "effectively arrogated to itself the right of first publication, an important marketable subsidiary right." Why it is different: the case was about verbatim copying of unpublished copyrighted expression. Facts are not copyrightable, and a tracker republishing facts from public records asserts no right of first publication.

Source: opinion text, law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/471/539

Flock Safety vs. DeFlock: the closest real-world test

DeFlock is a crowdsourced map of ALPR cameras. On January 30, 2025, Flock Safety's outside counsel sent its creator a cease-and-desist letter. The letter did not allege hacking, trespass, or terms-of-service violations, and it did not challenge the mapping itself. It asserted trademark dilution by blurring and tarnishment (15 U.S.C. § 1125(c)) and false advertising (Lanham Act § 43(a)), demanding the creator stop using the name "DeFlock." Notably, the letter itself conceded the key point:

"While Flock does not object to the free dissemination of truthful information, your use of the Flock Marks as part of your brand DEFLOCK is being wrongfully used to make false statements about Flock and its products and is damaging both its reputation and the goodwill associated with the Flock Marks."

On February 14, 2025, the Electronic Frontier Foundation responded on the creator's behalf:

"The claims alleged in your letter are groundless, and Mr. Freeman will not be complying with your demands."

The EFF's grounds: federal anti-dilution law expressly carves out noncommercial use and criticism or commentary on the mark owner (15 U.S.C. § 1125(c)(3)); dilution requires a "famous," household-name mark; and false-advertising law applies only to "commercial advertising or promotion," which a noncommercial camera map is not.

The outcome, stated precisely: DeFlock was not vindicated by a court order; after the EFF's rebuttal, no lawsuit followed and the site stayed up. And the dispute was about the project's name and branding, never about the lawfulness of publishing camera locations.

Sources: cease-and-desist letter, eff.org (PDF); EFF response letter, eff.org (PDF); reporting by 404 Media, 404media.co

The law that prohibits violence, vandalism, and threats

1. Destruction of government property: 18 U.S.C. § 1361

Whoever willfully injures or commits any depredation against any property of the United States, or of any department or agency thereof, or any property which has been or is being manufactured or constructed for the United States, or any department or agency thereof, or attempts to commit any of the foregoing offenses, shall be punished as follows: If the damage or attempted damage to such property exceeds the sum of $1,000, by a fine under this title or imprisonment for not more than ten years, or both; if the damage or attempted damage to such property does not exceed the sum of $1,000, by a fine under this title or by imprisonment for not more than one year, or both.

Source: law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/18/1361

Note: most Flock cameras are owned by Flock Safety or by state and local agencies rather than the federal government, so this specific statute may not cover a given camera. Every state separately criminalizes vandalism and destruction of property under its own criminal mischief, malicious destruction, or vandalism statutes. Whatever a given camera's ownership, damaging it is a crime under the applicable state law.

2. Threats transmitted online: 18 U.S.C. § 875(c)

Whoever transmits in interstate or foreign commerce any communication containing any threat to kidnap any person or any threat to injure the person of another, shall be fined under this title or imprisoned not more than five years, or both.

Source: law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/18/875

Federal courts have treated internet transmissions as satisfying the interstate-commerce element in section 875(c) prosecutions. Whether any given message violates the statute depends on its content and context, but posting threats online in connection with this cause can be a federal crime.
