## Page text

### Violence is never acceptable

Flock-Off is a transparency project. We document public contracts between
police agencies and Flock Safety so communities can debate them, file records
requests, pack council meetings, and vote. That is the entire playbook.

Violence is not acceptable under any circumstances. Not against people, not
against cameras, not against property, not as retaliation, not as protest.
Specifically:

- Do not damage, disable, deface, steal, or tamper with cameras or any related
  equipment.
- Do not threaten, harass, stalk, or dox any person, including Flock Safety
  employees, police officers, elected officials, or private citizens.
- Do not use information on this site to facilitate any of the above.

Anyone who commits violence or vandalism in the name of this cause is acting
against this project, not for it. We will not defend it, excuse it, or
characterize it as activism. Change the contract, not the hardware.

### The law that protects this publication

Publishing truthful information about government contracts and matters of
public concern is protected speech. Six authorities, quoted verbatim:

**1. The First Amendment to the United States Constitution**

> Congress shall make no law respecting an establishment of religion, or
> prohibiting the free exercise thereof; or abridging the freedom of speech,
> or of the press; or the right of the people peaceably to assemble, and to
> petition the Government for a redress of grievances.

Source: National Archives transcription of the Bill of Rights,
https://www.archives.gov/founding-docs/bill-of-rights-transcript

**2. New York Times Co. v. United States, 403 U.S. 713, 714 (1971) (per curiam)**

The Supreme Court refused to block publication of the Pentagon Papers,
holding:

> "Any system of prior restraints of expression comes to this Court bearing
> a heavy presumption against its constitutional validity."

Source: opinion text, https://www.law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/403/713
(transcription cross-checked at
http://law2.umkc.edu/faculty/projects/ftrials/ellsberg/nytvus.html)

**3. Bartnicki v. Vopper, 532 U.S. 514 (2001)**

The Court held the First Amendment protects publishing truthful information
about a matter of public concern:

> "Parallel reasoning requires the conclusion that a stranger's illegal
> conduct does not suffice to remove the First Amendment shield from speech
> about a matter of public concern."

Source: opinion syllabus,
https://www.law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/532/514

**4. Cox Broadcasting Corp. v. Cohn, 420 U.S. 469, 495 (1975)**

The Court held that states may not punish the publication of truthful
information contained in official records open to public inspection:

> "the States may not impose sanctions on the publication of truthful
> information contained in official court records open to public
> inspection."

Sources: opinion text,
https://www.law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/420/469 and
https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/420/469/

**5. The Florida Star v. B.J.F., 491 U.S. 524, 524-525 (1989)**

The Court held that truthful, lawfully obtained information about a matter
of public significance cannot be punished absent a state interest of the
highest order:

> "if a newspaper lawfully obtains truthful information about a matter of
> public significance then state officials may not constitutionally punish
> publication of the information, absent a need to further a state interest
> of the highest order."

Sources: official U.S. Reports PDF,
https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USREPORTS-491/pdf/USREPORTS-491-524.pdf
and https://www.law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/491/524

**6. Smith v. Daily Mail Publishing Co., 443 U.S. 97, 102 (1979)**

The Court stated the core rule later called the "Daily Mail principle":

> "Our recent decisions demonstrate that state action to punish the
> publication of truthful information seldom can satisfy constitutional
> standards."

Sources: https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/443/97/ and
https://caselaw.findlaw.com/court/us-supreme-court/443/97.html

These protections cover the *publication* of truthful, lawfully obtained
information. They do not create a right to obtain government records in the
first place, and each case was decided on its own facts rather than as
blanket immunity. Flock-Off stays inside these holdings by publishing only
information gathered lawfully: public records requests, open meetings, and
published documents. No hacking, no evasion of access controls.

### When courts have ordered takedowns, and why those cases are different

The protection for publishing truthful information is strong, but it is not
absolute. Courts have ordered published material removed in narrow
circumstances. Each of those cases turned on something this project does not
do:

**7. United States v. Progressive, Inc., 467 F. Supp. 990, 996 (W.D. Wis. 1979)**

A federal court enjoined a magazine from publishing technical details of
hydrogen bomb design, holding the article fell "within the extremely narrow
exception to the rule against prior restraint." The restraint rested on the
Atomic Energy Act's criminal prohibition on disclosing nuclear "Restricted
Data" found to pose grave, direct, immediate, and irreparable harm to the
United States. **Why it is different:** no federal statute prohibits
publishing public-records information about police contracts, and no court
has found such publication a national-security harm.

**8. Universal City Studios, Inc. v. Corley, 273 F.3d 429, 435 (2d Cir. 2001)**

The Second Circuit affirmed a permanent injunction barring the posting of,
and knowingly linking to, the DeCSS DVD-decryption program under the DMCA's
anti-trafficking provisions:

> "The injunction primarily bars the Appellants from posting DeCSS on their
> web site and from knowingly linking their web site to any other web site on
> which DeCSS is posted. We affirm."

**Why it is different:** the injunction targeted distribution of a functional
circumvention tool built to defeat copyright protection, not the
republication of truthful facts drawn from public records.

**9. DVD Copy Control Assn. v. Bunner, 31 Cal. 4th 864, 884 (2003)**

A trial court ordered a website operator to remove posted DeCSS code on
trade-secret grounds. The California Supreme Court held that such an
injunction, when proper under trade-secret law, is no First Amendment
violation:

> "Because the injunction is content neutral and was issued because of
> Bunner's prior unlawful conduct, we conclude it is not a prior restraint
> and therefore does not violate the First Amendment."

The full story matters: on remand the injunction was reversed for failure of
proof under trade-secret law, so the removal order did not ultimately stand.
**Why it is different:** the entire theory was misappropriation of
proprietary trade secrets the defendant knew were improperly acquired.
Information drawn from public government records cannot be anyone's trade
secret.

**10. Harper & Row v. Nation Enterprises, 471 U.S. 539, 545 (1985)**

This is the canonical case where a publisher lost, stated precisely: the
relief was damages ($12,500), not a takedown order. The Nation published 300
to 400 words of verbatim excerpts from President Ford's unpublished memoir to
scoop a licensed serialization, and the Court held the magazine "effectively
arrogated to itself the right of first publication, an important marketable
subsidiary right." **Why it is different:** the case was about verbatim
copying of unpublished copyrighted expression. Facts are not copyrightable,
and a tracker republishing facts from public records asserts no right of
first publication.

### Flock Safety vs. DeFlock: the closest real-world test

DeFlock is a crowdsourced map of ALPR cameras. On January 30, 2025, Flock
Safety's outside counsel sent its creator a cease-and-desist letter. The
letter did not allege hacking, trespass, or terms-of-service violations, and
it did not challenge the mapping itself. It asserted trademark dilution by
blurring and tarnishment (15 U.S.C. section 1125(c)) and false advertising
(Lanham Act section 43(a)), demanding the creator stop using the name
"DeFlock." Notably, the letter itself conceded the key point:

> "While Flock does not object to the free dissemination of truthful
> information, your use of the Flock Marks as part of your brand DEFLOCK is
> being wrongfully used to make false statements about Flock and its products
> and is damaging both its reputation and the goodwill associated with the
> Flock Marks."

On February 14, 2025, the Electronic Frontier Foundation responded on the
creator's behalf:

> "The claims alleged in your letter are groundless, and Mr. Freeman will
> not be complying with your demands."

The EFF's grounds: federal anti-dilution law expressly carves out
noncommercial use and criticism or commentary on the mark owner
(15 U.S.C. section 1125(c)(3)); dilution requires a "famous," household-name
mark; and false-advertising law applies only to "commercial advertising or
promotion," which a noncommercial camera map is not.

**The outcome, stated precisely:** DeFlock was not vindicated by a court
order; after the EFF's rebuttal, no lawsuit followed and the site stayed up. And the dispute was about the project's name and branding, never
about the lawfulness of publishing camera locations.

### The law that prohibits violence, vandalism, and threats

**1. Destruction of government property: 18 U.S.C. section 1361**

> Whoever willfully injures or commits any depredation against any property
> of the United States, or of any department or agency thereof, or any
> property which has been or is being manufactured or constructed for the
> United States, or any department or agency thereof, or attempts to commit
> any of the foregoing offenses, shall be punished as follows: If the damage
> or attempted damage to such property exceeds the sum of $1,000, by a fine
> under this title or imprisonment for not more than ten years, or both; if
> the damage or attempted damage to such property does not exceed the sum of
> $1,000, by a fine under this title or by imprisonment for not more than
> one year, or both.

Source: https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/18/1361

Note: most Flock cameras are owned by Flock Safety or by state and local
agencies rather than the federal government, so this specific statute may not
cover a given camera. Every state separately criminalizes vandalism and
destruction of property under its own criminal mischief, malicious
destruction, or vandalism statutes. Whatever a given camera's ownership,
damaging it is a crime under the applicable state law.

**2. Threats transmitted online: 18 U.S.C. section 875(c)**

> Whoever transmits in interstate or foreign commerce any communication
> containing any threat to kidnap any person or any threat to injure the
> person of another, shall be fined under this title or imprisoned not more
> than five years, or both.

Source: https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/18/875

Federal courts have treated internet transmissions as satisfying the
interstate-commerce element in section 875(c) prosecutions. Whether any
given message violates the statute depends on its content and context, but
posting threats online in connection with this cause can be a federal
crime.
