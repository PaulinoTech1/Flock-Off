I checked several of the points you raised, and Nevada is becoming one of the clearest examples of how quickly this debate is changing. Public records show at least 180 automated plate readers in Washoe County, yet Nevada still does not have a specific statewide law regulating how these systems are used or how long the information can be retained. Now lawmakers are considering two very different approaches. One proposal would establish guardrails — written policies, user training, reporting requirements and independent audits. Assemblymember Selena La Rue Hatch is going further and has announced plans for legislation that would seek to ban automated license-plate-reader networks such as Flock statewide. And the political divide is interesting because it isn't a simple Democrat-versus-Republican fight. Attorney General Aaron Ford has said he does not support the current use of Flock without stringent protections against abuse, while Gov. Joe Lombardo has emphasized the public-safety benefits but also acknowledged the need for safeguards. That's exactly the argument we've been following throughout this series: Do we regulate this technology — or has the network already become too powerful to regulate safely? The Washoe County numbers especially caught my attention, because 180 cameras in one northern Nevada county turns this from a discussion about an occasional roadside camera into a discussion about an actual surveillance network.
