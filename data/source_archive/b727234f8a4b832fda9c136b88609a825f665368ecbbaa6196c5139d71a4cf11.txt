State Wide Wilder Amends Flock Camera Deal, But Idahoans Still Want It Gone Local Yocal Idaho Jul 20, 2026 ∙ Paid 3 1 Share Upgrade to paid to play voiceover Photo by Mother Flocker on Unsplash The Wilder City Council voted Tuesday night to amend its Flock Safety camera contract, blocking the company from using town data to train its facial-recognition and machine-learning systems, but Idahoans who wanted the cameras gone entirely walked away with nothing. What the Council Changed Wilder Idaho News 6 reporter Victoria Rodriguez reported on July 14, 2026 , that the council approved a change which limited how Flock Safety can use license-plate data collected by the cameras in town. The amendment prevents the company from feeding Wilder’s data into its broader product-development and machine-learning process, though it leaves the underlying contract and the cameras still in place. A Flock representative told the council the machine-learning process never touches identifiable customer data and that the company’s cameras read plates, not faces, though that wasn’t the issue people were having. Share Residents Push Back Again Several locals at the meeting asked the council to scrap the deal completely rather than patch it, arguing the original agreement never got a full council vote and could expose the town to lawsuits. One local said during the meeting that, “ I don’t want to be anybody’s product, “ raised an issue with who controls footage of Wilder’s streets and where it ends up. Others raised fears about cameras near Wilder Community Park catching images of kids at play, and about Fourth Amendment limits on tracking people who haven’t committed a crime. A Pattern Building in Canyon County This fight didn’t start on Tuesday. Local Yocal Idaho reported in April that Wilder local, Tyler Cain, launched a voter initiative to strip the cameras out entirely after the town installed them without a public ordinance. That same report covered that former Jerome County Sheriff George Oppedyke, resigned after running more than 1,000 plate searches on his wife’s car. Proof that tools aren’t the issue, but unchecked people who invite abuse. Rodriguez also noted a separate tort claim accusing Caldwell’s Flock program of violating Idaho privacy law, showing the pushback stretches beyond one small town. Subscribe Why This Still Matters Recording a car on a public street breaks no law, and catching real criminals matters. But when a private tech firm, not the town, decides how footage flows, and a council can quietly bind a town to fees for years, that keeps eroding the trust in local civil governments that we are already fighting against in a low-trust society. Cain’s initiative, aimed at the 2027 ballot, remains the clearest test of whether Wilder voters, not just the council, get final say.
