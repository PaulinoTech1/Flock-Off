Subscribe In October 2014, journalist Glenn Greenwald stepped onto the TEDGlobal stage in Rio de Janeiro and confronted one of the most seductive arguments ever offered in defense of government surveillance: If you have nothing to hide, why should you care if government watches you? Greenwald’s Why Privacy Matters came in the aftermath of Edward Snowden’s extraordinary disclosure of the National Security Agency’s surveillance capabilities. Snowden provided journalists, including Greenwald, classified documents revealing surveillance programs most Americans never knew existed. Whatever judgment one makes about Snowden’s decision to disclose classified information, one consequence is undeniable: He turned on the lights. He forced Americans to confront surveillance capabilities their government had developed largely outside public view. Greenwald then explained why that matters. Privacy isn’t primarily about concealing wrongdoing. It is about maintaining the private space necessary for human freedom. People who believe they may be watched behave differently. Surveillance encourages caution, conformity and awareness of authority. Twelve years later, Oklahoma should revisit Greenwald’s warning. Because the surveillance apparatus is no longer something abstract operating inside a distant federal intelligence agency. It is mounted beside Oklahoma roads. FROM INSURANCE ENFORCEMENT TO SOMETHING MUCH BIGGER Automatic License Plate Readers, ALPRs, photograph passing vehicles, identify license plates and associate those observations with locations and times. A single photograph tells government relatively little. Thousands or millions of searchable observations can reveal considerably more. Where someone works. Where they worship. Which political meetings they attend. Which doctors they visit. Whose homes they frequent. Where they spend their evenings. Oklahoma law expressly authorized ALPR technology in connection with the state’s Uninsured Vehicle Enforcement Program. Title 47, Section 7-606.1 authorizes participating law-enforcement agencies to use ALPR systems for the investigation, detection, analysis or enforcement of Oklahoma’s Compulsory Insurance Law. Then the Legislature imposed an important limitation. Information collected or retained through an ALPR system cannot be used for purposes other than compulsory-insurance enforcement “or as otherwise permitted by law.” Those final words matter. They mean it would be an overstatement to claim every conceivable non-insurance use is automatically unlawful. But they also create an obvious burden for government: Show us the other law. If a sheriff wants to search someone’s historical movements, identify the statute authorizing it. If a police department wants to use ALPR data to investigate crimes unrelated to insurance, identify the authority. If local government wants to share citizens’ location information across jurisdictions or through nationwide databases, show Oklahomans where their elected Legislature authorized it. “As otherwise permitted by law” cannot simply become: Whatever government finds useful. And that brings Oklahoma to an old principle of limited government that deserves renewed attention. DILLON’S RULE SHOW US THE POWER Oklahoma has long applied Dillon’s Rule, particularly when defining county authority. The principle turns modern government’s increasingly common assumption on its head. A county doesn’t possess every power the Legislature has failed to prohibit. It possesses powers expressly granted by law, powers necessarily or fairly implied from those grants, and powers indispensable, not merely convenient, to carrying out its authorized functions. Oklahoma authority goes further: reasonable doubt concerning whether county government possesses a particular power is resolved against the existence of that power. That distinction is critical. The question isn’t: Where does Oklahoma law prohibit Cleveland County from constructing a searchable vehicle-surveillance network? The question is: Where does Oklahoma law authorize it? Home-rule municipalities present a somewhat different legal analysis because Oklahoma’s Constitution affords charter cities substantial authority over municipal affairs. But home rule isn’t a blank check to disregard state statutes involving matters of statewide concern. The broader limited-government principle remains: Government should establish its lawful authority before exercising power over citizens, not force citizens to prove afterward that government never possessed it. Now follow Oklahoma’s ALPR trail. GUTHRIE: THE EXPERIMENT ENDS — BUT THE CAMERAS DON’T Guthrie provides one of the strangest chapters in Oklahoma’s ALPR story because the city eventually did something governments seldom do with surveillance technology: It declined to renew it. Guthrie approved its Flock program in November 2022. A subsequent 24-month agreement placed 12 cameras into service using approximately $61,000 in federal grant funding. When that money disappeared, taxpayers faced the question that eventually follows nearly every grant-funded government program: Are local citizens willing to pay to continue it? Continuing Flock would have cost roughly $35,000 annually. The city decided not to renew. Police Chief Don Sweger notified Flock in February 2025 that Guthrie did not intend to continue the service. That should have ended the story. It did not. Open Records Act requests later revealed something extraordinary. Guthrie Clerk/Treasurer Kim Biggs stated that the city had “no formal or informal agreements, renewals, or amendments” authorizing continued camera use beyond the original agreement. The city had not authorized continued operation, and according to Biggs, Flock had repeatedly been asked to remove its equipment. Yet Flock’s own transparency portal continued showing cameras operating months later. As late as July 2025, the portal reportedly showed 10 cameras active, tens of thousands of vehicle detections, dozens of hot-list alerts and numerous searches. The cameras were eventually removed. Think about that sequence. The elected government authorized surveillance. The funding ended. The city declined renewal. The police chief communicated that decision. City officials said no subsequent agreement authorized continued operation. Yet the surveillance infrastructure apparently continued collecting information. That is more than a contracting problem. It is surveillance inertia. The camera doesn’t know the contract expired. The database doesn’t know elected officials declined another appropriation. The software doesn’t understand Dillon’s Rule. Technology doesn’t stop and ask whether government still possesses lawful authority before recording another citizen’s movements. People must impose those limits. EDMOND: THE PILOT BECOMES INFRASTRUCTURE Edmond followed a familiar pattern. The city launched a Flock pilot in 2023. By 2024, Edmond Police had contracted for 10 ALPRs and subsequently sought renewal and additional cameras. The advertised purposes extended well beyond uninsured motorists: stolen vehicles, wanted persons, missing people, AMBER Alerts and Silver Alerts. Those are sympathetic purposes. But a worthwhile governmental objective does not manufacture governmental authority. Questions about transparency followed, including reluctance to disclose camera locations. The experiment became infrastructure. That is how surveillance often grows. A pilot. A grant. Ten cameras. Four more. A renewal. Another database. Another agency receiving access. Eventually yesterday’s extraordinary surveillance becomes today’s routine policing. McCLAIN COUNTY: AN OKLAHOMA JUDGE SAYS NO Then Oklahoma’s ALPR controversy reached a courtroom. In State of Oklahoma v. Ifabiyi , a Norman police officer used ALPR information during an investigation involving Kayode Ifabiyi and Ngoc Nguyen. The database search produced historical vehicle-location information that became part of the investigation. Police ultimately discovered a handgun and nearly $100,000 in cash. Those facts make the case uncomfortable for defenders of constitutional restraints. As they should. Constitutional and statutory protections aren’t reserved for sympathetic defendants. The McClain County District Court granted a motion to suppress. The court concluded that the challenged ALPR use fell outside the purposes permitted under Oklahoma law. That ruling matters. It moves this debate beyond hypothetical privacy concerns. An Oklahoma judge has already suppressed evidence following unlawful ALPR use. Precision matters, however. This was a district-court ruling in one case, not binding statewide appellate precedent. Federal courts also aren’t necessarily required to suppress evidence simply because a state officer violated an Oklahoma statute. Federal suppression generally turns on federal constitutional standards. But neither qualification answers the fundamental state-law question: What Oklahoma law authorizes general-purpose, non-insurance ALPR searches? NORMAN SAID NO CLEVELAND COUNTY SAID YES Norman considered Flock and rejected it. City leaders expressed concerns about recording vehicles belonging to people suspected of absolutely nothing and retaining those observations in a private company’s database. Norman’s elected municipal government said no. Then Cleveland County effectively said yes. The Cleveland County Sheriff’s Office deployed Flock cameras, including cameras operating within Norman. In 2026, county commissioners approved a renewal involving 20 cameras after repeatedly postponing the issue while awaiting legal guidance. One commissioner publicly characterized the legal question as “super gray” and suggested the Legislature needed to address it. That statement should stop every taxpayer in his tracks. Under Dillon’s Rule, uncertainty concerning county power isn’t necessarily permission to proceed. It may mean exactly the opposite. If county officials themselves believe their authority occupies a legally gray area, the limited-government solution shouldn’t be: Spend taxpayer money now and wait for somebody to sue us. It should be: Obtain unmistakable legislative authority first. Norman citizens therefore encountered a remarkable version of representative government. Their city government rejected Flock. County government brought the surveillance network into their community anyway. OKLAHOMA CITY THE THEORETICAL ABUSE BECOMES REAL Meanwhile, Oklahoma City constructed a much larger Flock network. Then citizens began asking questions. And eventually came the revelation surveillance advocates continually assure the public it shouldn’t worry about: Someone misused it. An Oklahoma City police sergeant improperly conducted personal database searches over a period extending from December 2024 through April 2025, including improper Flock searches. The misconduct resulted in a three-day unpaid suspension and one year of disciplinary probation. Perhaps most troubling, the misconduct was uncovered following a citizen complaint, not because an automated internal auditing system discovered it. Chief Ron Bacy subsequently acknowledged two employee incidents involving Flock. That matters enormously. The argument that surveillance abuse is merely theoretical is over. That isn’t an indictment of every police officer. It is an acknowledgment of human nature. Human beings make mistakes. Human beings become curious. Human beings sometimes abuse authority. America’s constitutional system was designed around precisely that reality. THEN OKLAHOMA CITY ADDED FACES Flock wasn’t Oklahoma City’s only venture into powerful surveillance technology. In 2025, Oklahoma City also contracted with Clearview AI, purchasing facial-comparison technology capable of allowing investigators to submit a photograph and search for potential facial matches against the company’s enormous image database. Police emphasized that a Clearview match would be treated as an investigative lead rather than probable cause for an arrest. That safeguard matters. It doesn’t eliminate the larger issue. Clearview built its facial-search capability using billions of images gathered from publicly accessible internet sources, including social-media platforms. Oklahoma City therefore wasn’t merely purchasing another piece of police software. It was purchasing access to technology capable of identifying human faces. The Clearview agreement was expected to expire around June 2026, according to the contract timeline The Sooner Sentinel has been following, while other publicly available information has described Oklahoma City’s access as extending into July 2026. Either way, that stated contractual period has passed. Yet as of publication, The Sooner Sentinel has not received confirmation from Oklahoma City establishing whether its Clearview AI contract actually terminated. We are not claiming Clearview remains active. We are asking Oklahoma City to answer a straightforward question: Is Oklahoma City Police still using Clearview AI? Was the agreement allowed to expire? Was it extended? Was it replaced? Are police accounts still active? Is Oklahoma City now using another facial-comparison system? And the timing raises another legitimate question. The Clearview agreement reached its expected expiration period shortly before Oklahoma City faced an increasingly contentious public battle over renewing Flock. Then, on August 18, the City Council renewed Flock. So where is Clearview? Did Oklahoma City discontinue facial recognition? Or is another Clearview agreement coming now that the politically difficult Flock renewal is safely behind the council? We don’t know. And it would be irresponsible to claim otherwise. But Oklahoma City officials do know. If Clearview was terminated, say so. If it was renewed, taxpayers should see the agreement. If access continues under another arrangement, citizens deserve to know under what authority and at what cost. And if another Clearview contract is headed toward the City Council after the Flock controversy subsides, citizens deserve advance notice and a public debate. Guthrie has already demonstrated why Oklahomans shouldn’t assume a contract expiration automatically means surveillance stopped. OKC RENEWS FLOCK BUT READ THE FINE PRINT On August 18, 2026, Oklahoma City’s council voted 5-3 to renew Flock for another year, approving approximately $270,000 for roughly 90 cameras. Nearly 30 citizens spoke before the vote. Those speaking opposed renewal. Chief Bacy defended the technology with substantial numbers: 1,280 recovered vehicles, 210 missing people located and assistance in 60 murder cases. Those accomplishments deserve acknowledgment. But effectiveness does not establish legality. Government could undoubtedly solve more crimes by searching every house without a warrant. It could solve more crimes by tracking every cellphone. It could solve more crimes by placing facial-recognition cameras throughout Oklahoma City. That doesn’t mean a free society should permit it. And another aspect of the renewal deserves closer scrutiny. OKCPD reduced ordinary Flock data retention from 30 days to seven. The department also turned off routine participation in Flock’s nationwide sharing network. Those are meaningful improvements. But citizens should understand the difference between a contractual prohibition and an administrative setting. Available information indicates Oklahoma City’s withdrawal from routine nationwide sharing is not necessarily an irreversible contractual requirement. It is something OKCPD has chosen to turn off. Chief Bacy acknowledged during the August 18 meeting that broader sharing could be activated for significant incidents on a case-by-case basis. In other words: Oklahoma City turned the switch off. It apparently didn’t remove the switch. The contract amendment provides a different protection: Flock itself is restricted from sharing Oklahoma City’s information with federal agencies without City authorization. That is meaningful. But it is not the same thing as permanently disconnecting Oklahoma City from broader information-sharing capabilities. That raises an obvious question: Who has the power to turn Oklahoma City back on? If police administrators can alter national-sharing settings without another City Council vote, citizens deserve to know who possesses that authority, what circumstances justify exercising it, how long the sharing may continue and whether the public will ever be notified. The same scrutiny should apply to retention. Seven days is better than 30. But if retention is an operational setting rather than a binding contractual limit, who can change it back? That isn’t paranoia. It is oversight. And Oklahoma City’s documented employee misuse demonstrates exactly why oversight matters. FROM READING PLATES TO IDENTIFYING FACES Now consider the larger picture. Oklahoma City acquired technology capable of identifying vehicles and recording where they travel. It separately purchased technology capable of comparing human faces against an enormous photographic database. There is no evidence presented here that Flock and Clearview are technically integrated, and they should not be portrayed as though they are. The concern is more fundamental: The same government acquired both capabilities. One technology identifies vehicles. Another identifies faces. And citizens must ask what government becomes capable of doing as surveillance technologies accumulate. License plate. Vehicle description. Location. Time. Photograph. Face. Identity. That is no longer merely a license-plate-reader debate. It is a debate about the architecture of government surveillance. GREENWALD’S WARNING HAS ARRIVED IN OKLAHOMA And that brings us back to Glenn Greenwald. The camera doesn’t know whether you are suspected of committing a crime. It photographs everyone. The teacher. The pastor. The gun owner. The journalist. The political activist. The legislator. The grandmother driving to church. The citizen attending a protest. The patient visiting a clinic. The innocent person simply driving home. Government collects the information first. It decides who becomes interesting later. That reverses the traditional logic of criminal investigation. Historically, police investigated crimes, developed evidence, identified suspects and directed investigative powers toward those suspects. Mass ALPR surveillance offers another model: Collect everybody’s movements now. Search for suspects later. Snowden showed Americans what can happen when surveillance capabilities grow faster than public understanding. Greenwald explained why citizens who have nothing to hide nevertheless have every reason to value privacy. And Oklahoma has supplied its own chronology. Guthrie adopted Flock, then declined to renew it, only to discover cameras apparently continued operating afterward. Edmond expanded it. A McClain County judge suppressed evidence following unlawful ALPR use. Norman rejected it. Cleveland County brought it into Norman anyway. Oklahoma City built a 90-camera network. Oklahoma City documented employee misuse. Oklahoma City separately purchased facial-comparison technology from Clearview AI. The stated Clearview contract period expired in the summer of 2026, yet Oklahoma City has not confirmed to The Sooner Sentinel whether the agreement terminated, was extended or was replaced. Then Oklahoma City renewed Flock. It reduced retention and turned off routine nationwide sharing, while apparently retaining administrative ability to change at least some of those settings. At some point, Oklahoma lawmakers must stop allowing cities, counties, sheriffs, police departments and private surveillance companies to determine the boundaries of governmental power through contracts, departmental policies, databases and software settings. If Oklahoma wants general-purpose ALPR surveillance, legislators should introduce that proposal publicly. Define what government may collect. Define who may search it. Define how long information may be retained. Define who may change those limits. Restrict sharing. Require warrants where constitutionally appropriate. Require independent audits. Create meaningful penalties for misuse. And make every legislator put his or her name beside the vote. Until then, § 7-606.1 still says what it says. And Dillon’s Rule still asks the question county government cannot simply wish away: Where is the authority? Edward Snowden turned on the light. Glenn Greenwald explained why Americans should look. Oklahoma’s experience shows why we should keep looking. Because the central danger isn’t merely what government says it is doing with surveillance technology today. It is what government has built the capability to do tomorrow. In a free society, citizens should never have to explain why they don’t want government recording their movements or identifying their faces. Government should have to explain, and prove, its lawful authority for watching them. Watch Glenn Greenwald’s “Why Privacy Matters” Thanks for reading!
