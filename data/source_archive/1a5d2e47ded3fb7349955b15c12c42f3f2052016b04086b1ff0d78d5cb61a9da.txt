Photo: Smith Collection/Gado/Getty Images Scottsdale is considering cutting ties with Flock Safety while exploring other automated license plate reader (ALPR) vendors. Why it matters: The city joins a host of other local governments responding to the nationwide backlash over Flock's privacy practices. But the city remains committed to using the technology, even if another company provides it. Driving the news: At a meeting Tuesday, City Council members unanimously voted to direct staff to look into other ALPR vendors and return Dec. 1 with a proposed ordinance for the use of ALPRs. Zoom in: The motion directed staff to exclude Flock from the proposed fiscal 2027-28 budget and figure out how to end its contract by June 30, 2027. Any exit depends on Scottsdale having a replacement system ready, meaning the cameras will continue to work during the transition. By the numbers: The Flock contract runs through June 2028 and costs about $280,000 annually. Scottsdale's 85 cameras record when and where vehicles pass them. The city retains data for 185 days. The other side: Flock supports the city in "considering additional guardrails" to strengthen privacy and accountability and is willing to work with the city to "ensure the technology is used responsibly," a Flock spokesperson tells Axios. State of play: From January through August, the cameras helped city police locate five vulnerable people, per the department. The department's real-time crime center receives 50 to 100 Flock alerts daily but acts on fewer than 5%. Audits by the police department found no misuse by Scottsdale officers, police said. Yes, but: The council directed the city auditor to begin an initial audit within 90 days and recommend whether a specialized outside auditor is needed. Zoom out: About 65 Arizona law enforcement agencies can request access to Scottsdale data to help track suspects, stolen vehicles and more. Police chief Joe LeDuc says access is limited to Arizona law enforcement agencies with certified lawful use of ALPRs and policies comparable to Scottsdale's. What they're saying: "Please don't simply replace Flock with another company's version of the same technology," a resident said during public comment Tuesday. The big picture: Several Arizona communities have ended or suspended their Flock programs after reports of potential misuse and growing privacy concerns.
